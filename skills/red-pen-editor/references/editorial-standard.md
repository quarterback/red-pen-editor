# Editorial review rubric (v0.1)

## Priority order

1. **Necessary meaning:** What new information, qualification, or action does the span contribute?
2. **Structure:** Is its position useful? Does the lead answer the question? Does the conclusion merely repeat it?
3. **Selection:** Can an entire paragraph or section disappear without sacrificing purpose?
4. **Precision:** Can the remaining sentence say the same thing more clearly?
5. **Voice:** Does the revision still sound like the source author?

A good cut scores highly on selection while remaining safe on meaning and voice. Length reduction is a secondary measurement, never the goal.

## Edit categories

- `duplicate`: same information appears elsewhere, verbatim or nearly so
- `redundancy`: repeated idea with different wording
- `throat-clearing`: preface, apology, or transition that postpones the point
- `overexplaining`: explanation beyond audience need, with no new evidence
- `structure`: unnecessary section, heading, summary, or reordered statement
- `line-edit`: replace wordy construction with direct phrasing
- `other`: contextual editorial concern

## Example 1: remove an empty opener

Before:

> There are several important aspects of the proposed permit change that residents may want to be aware of. Starting May 1, residents must apply online. Paper applications remain available at the library.

After:

> Starting May 1, residents must apply online. Paper applications remain available at the library.

Reason: The first sentence adds nothing that readers need to act.

## Example 2: select rather than compress

Before:

> The committee met three times in June. Each meeting included an update about the revised service plan. The revised service plan was discussed at every meeting. Members approved the plan on June 28.

After:

> The committee discussed the revised service plan at three June meetings and approved it on June 28.

Reason: Two sentences restated the meeting agenda; the revision retains the number of meetings and the decision date.

## Example 3: preserve a necessary condition

Before:

> The system may produce an answer when sufficient records are available, but staff must review it before sending a notice.

Bad edit:

> The system produces answers automatically.

Reason: This changes uncertainty and removes human review, both central to the claim.

## Review checklist

- Does the first paragraph perform real work?
- Does each later paragraph contribute new information?
- Is any example followed by a paragraph that only repeats its lesson?
- Does a conclusion repeat the opening without an additional decision or consequence?
- Are qualifiers actually expressing uncertainty or limits?
- Has the editor introduced a stronger assertion, altered causality, or dropped attribution?
- Would the writer recognize their own register after the edit?
- Did the revision remove text that should have stayed?

## Editorial foundations

- Strunk and White, *The Elements of Style*: omit needless words, used with discretion.
- George Orwell, *Politics and the English Language*: avoid stale phrases and unnecessary abstract language.
- U.S. Federal Plain Language Guidelines: familiar words, clear responsibilities, direct language.
  https://www.plainlanguage.gov/guidelines/
- GOV.UK content design guidance: content should be useful, specific, and easy to scan.
  https://www.gov.uk/guidance/content-design/writing-for-gov-uk
- Purdue OWL, Conciseness: eliminate needless words while preserving meaning.
  https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/conciseness/index.html
- Roy Peter Clark, Poynter: editing through selection rather than mechanical compression.

These are influences, not a single mandatory house style. The agent applies judgment to the specific text.
